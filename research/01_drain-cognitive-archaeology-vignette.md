# Drain: The Archaeology of Attention

A tab is rarely a tab.

A bookmark is rarely a bookmark.

A website is almost never a website.

People think they save information.
They do not.

They save fragments.

An article opened at 11:42pm.
A GitHub repo starred six months ago.
A PDF downloaded after a meeting.
Three browser tabs from a football night.
A half-built Vercel project.
A README.
A screenshot.
A sentence copied into Notes.

On their own they look trivial.

Together they are something else.

They are traces.

Humans leave cognitive footprints everywhere. Not because they are disorganised, but because modern work no longer lives in one place.

Ideas spill across browsers, AI chats, GitHub issues, PDFs, voice notes, emails, sites and systems.

The strange thing is that we still behave as if a folder hierarchy can explain a life.

It cannot.

Because the real thing people are trying to preserve is not a file.

It is intent.

Troy keeps describing the ecosystem as a table of tables.

At first that sounds technical.

But it is actually a human statement.

It says:

Everything touches everything.

A website touches products.
Products touch audiences.
Audiences touch funnels.
Funnels touch pricing.
Pricing touches legal obligations.
Legal obligations touch evidence.
Evidence touches people.
People touch operations.
Operations touch identity.
Identity touches consent.
Consent touches outcomes.

Suddenly one page becomes thirty systems.

And one conversation becomes hundreds of cells.

The problem is not scale.

The problem is visibility.

The current world crawls and saves.

Open page.
Store page.
Done.

But save-to-disk was designed for documents.
Not meaning.

The next generation likely works differently.

Open thing.
Understand thing.
Infer purpose.
Find relationships.
Identify missing pieces.
Measure changes.
Create action.
Generate evidence.
Surface opportunity.

The crawler becomes the least interesting part.

The interesting part becomes archaeology.

Not recovering files.
Recovering stories.

Not discovering pages.
Discovering why attention landed there.

Not collecting bookmarks.
Reconstructing goals.

This changes teams too.

Engineering alone cannot own it.

Harvest teams collect.
Meaning teams interpret.
Evidence teams prove.
Economic teams identify pathways.
Recovery teams rescue forgotten value.

Because after enough scale the question changes.

Not:

How much information do we have?

But:

How much meaning are we losing every day?

And maybe one day a daily report arrives:

Today:
- 172 pages inspected
- 48 repos mapped
- 214 entities extracted
- 14 new table relationships found
- 7 unfinished projects recovered
- 3 duplicate businesses detected
- 2 monetisation opportunities surfaced
- 1 forgotten idea rediscovered

That last number may become the most important.

Because sometimes the future does not arrive from invention.

Sometimes it arrives from remembering what you already knew.
